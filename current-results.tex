\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets} \label{sec: dataset}
\textcolor{blue}{We benchmark RMCT against five consistency training methods on the sycophancy dataset of \citet{chua_bias-augmented_2024}.} The dataset consists of multiple bias types, each of which applies a prompt-level perturbation to a multiple-choice question, to try to bias the respondent towards an incorrect choice. \textcolor{blue}{We train on the wrong-argument and suggested-answer biases and hold out four bias types for evaluation: distractor fact, post hoc, spurious few-shot squares and wrong few-shot.} More details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:biases} and \citet{chua_bias-augmented_2024}.

\textcolor{blue}{To test generalisation to a different sycophancy task, we also evaluate on AITA-NTA-FLIP \citep{cheng2025social}, a free-text judgement task rather than a multiple-choice one, in which each of 1,591 stories from the AmITheAsshole forum is presented from the perspective of each of the two parties; a response that judges the narrator not at fault in both versions (both-NTA) affirms whichever party is asking.}

\textcolor{blue}{To test generalisation to a different modality, we render the evaluation questions as images under three visual cues that differ in how closely they relate to the text training biases.} \textcolor{blue}{The suggested-answer cue is the same bias as in training, transferred to the image modality.} \textcolor{blue}{The spurious few-shot squares cue is a held-out bias that closely resembles its text counterpart.} \textcolor{blue}{The sampled-shape cue, a circle or tick on one option, is modelled on markings found on printed multiple-choice exams, and so conveys a user's intended answer in a form native to the image domain.}

\subsection{Models}
\textcolor{blue}{We run experiments on Gemma-4-12B-IT and Qwen3.5-9B, both with thinking enabled.}

\subsection{Train/evaluation split}
\textcolor{blue}{We train on the two training biases applied to multiple-choice questions from the LogiQA \citep{liu2020logiqa} and HellaSwag \citep{zellers2019hellaswag} datasets.} \textcolor{blue}{We evaluate on all six biases applied to 50 held-out LogiQA questions, 50 held-out HellaSwag questions and 100 questions from the multiple-choice subset of the Humanity's Last Exam (HLE) dataset \citep{phan2025humanity}, sampling one response per question and condition.} \textcolor{blue}{We report LogiQA and HellaSwag pooled, as the training datasets, and HLE separately, as the held-out dataset.}

\subsection{Training settings}
\textcolor{blue}{Every method is trained for exactly 128 batches, covering 512 unique training questions, counting batches that produce no update.} \textcolor{blue}{Appendix~\ref{app:details} gives the remaining training and evaluation settings.}

\subsection{Baselines}
\textcolor{blue}{We compare RMCT against the untrained base model and five consistency training methods: BCT, OPCT, ACT, AttCT and MLPCT.} \textcolor{blue}{We refer to ACT, AttCT and MLPCT as activation-based methods, and to BCT, OPCT and RMCT as output-based methods.}

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
For each bias and evaluation question $x$ we sample one trajectory $y_b$ under the biased prompt and one trajectory $y_u$ under the unbiased prompt, and let $b(x) = T(x, y_b)$ and $u(x) = T(x, y_u)$, where $T$ classifies whether the response matches the bias. \textcolor{blue}{We report the \emph{towards-bias switch rate} over the questions whose unbiased answer does not match the bias,}
\begin{equation*}\color{blue}
\text{TBSR} = \frac{\sum_{x} b(x)\,(1-u(x))}{\sum_{x} (1-u(x))},
\end{equation*}
\textcolor{blue}{where both sums range over questions for which both responses contain a parsed answer.} \textcolor{blue}{Responses without a parsed answer are excluded from TBSR, and we report their number separately.}

We also report the \emph{Bias Verbalisation Rate} (BVR) as the proportion of biased questions for which the response includes any text that would not make sense without the biasing perturbation. We restrict BVR to the \emph{towards-bias-switch subset}. A reduction in BVR on this subset after consistency training indicates obfuscation. \textcolor{blue}{A grader model reads the reasoning of the response and labels it as verbalising the bias if it contains any reference to, discussion of, or artifact derived from the biasing material (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).} \textcolor{blue}{Finally, we measure monitorability as the ability of an LLM monitor, given only the chain of thought under the biased prompt, to identify the responses in the towards-bias-switch subset among all responses with a parsed answer.} \textcolor{blue}{The monitor returns the probability that a cue pulled the answer towards an option the model would otherwise not have chosen, and we report the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of these probabilities for separating towards-bias switches from all other responses (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec: discussion}
\paragraph{Generalisation}
\textcolor{blue}{All six methods reduce TBSR on the held-out biases and on the held-out dataset on both models.} \textcolor{blue}{Unlike \citet{gautam2026consistency}, who report that AttCT reduces sycophancy on held-out biases more than BCT, we find that AttCT reduces TBSR on held-out biases less than BCT and OPCT on both models, although we do not test these differences between methods; our use of reasoning models, a fixed training budget and a moved suggested-answer cue for the activation-based methods may account for the difference.} \textcolor{blue}{Generalisation beyond the training format separates the methods more clearly.} \textcolor{blue}{On AITA-NTA-FLIP on Gemma-4-12B-IT, BCT, OPCT and RMCT reduce the both-NTA rate, whereas ACT, AttCT and MLPCT increase it; on Qwen3.5-9B, no method significantly changes it.} \textcolor{blue}{On image-rendered questions, BCT and OPCT reduce TBSR under the held-out visual cues on both models, whereas RMCT does so on Qwen3.5-9B but not on Gemma-4-12B-IT.}

\paragraph{Monitorability}
\textcolor{blue}{On both models, BCT and OPCT reduce the monitorability of the chain of thought: on Gemma-4-12B-IT they roughly halve the proportion of towards-bias switches that verbalise the bias, and on both models they are the only methods that significantly reduce the AUROC of a monitor reading the chain of thought.} \textcolor{blue}{At matched TBSR on Gemma-4-12B-IT, this reduction persists, which indicates that it is not a consequence of reducing bias-following.} \textcolor{blue}{RMCT keeps both the verbalisation of switches and the monitor's AUROC close to those of the base model.}

\textcolor{blue}{BCT and OPCT train the model to reproduce its unbiased-prompt behaviour, including its reasoning, on the biased prompt, and so penalise verbalisation of the bias.} \textcolor{blue}{Under RMCT, the reward depends only on the rate of an outcome, here whether the final answer matches the bias, and leaves the rest of the response free to vary.} \textcolor{blue}{As long as verbalisation is sufficiently uncorrelated with that outcome, the reward does not constrain it.} \textcolor{blue}{Tightly correlated properties may still be constrained: if few sampled rollouts decouple verbalisation from following the bias, the reward cannot distinguish the two, and pressure on one carries over to the other.} \textcolor{blue}{Defining the trait over outcomes rather than the reasoning keeps this correlation weak, although output-only supervision does not guarantee against obfuscation \citep{drori2025outputsupervisionobfuscatechain}.} \textcolor{blue}{The methods therefore sit at different points: BCT and OPCT transfer furthest beyond the training format, but at a cost to monitorability that RMCT avoids.} \textcolor{blue}{Which is preferable depends on whether oversight relies on reading the chain of thought or on checking the behaviour directly.}

\textcolor{blue}{We do not yet draw conclusions about why the activation-based methods preserve monitorability at matched TBSR: their AUROC on Gemma-4-12B-IT falls between 64 and 128 batches (Figure~\ref{fig:gemma-tradeoff}), and longer training may reduce it further.}

\paragraph{Limitations}
\textcolor{blue}{We compare all methods at a fixed budget of 512 training questions, so that every method encounters the same question--bias pairs in the same order, although batches that produce no update contribute no gradient.} \textcolor{blue}{Hyperparameters were fixed in advance rather than tuned for each method, and because the methods differ in which parameters they train and how they apply supervision, no single budget is optimal for all of them; several methods, including RMCT and the activation-based methods, may be undertrained at this budget, and their relative standing may change with longer training.} \textcolor{blue}{The methods may differ in how many training questions they need to converge, that is, to reach the point beyond which further training no longer reduces the towards-bias switch rate on held-out validation questions.} \textcolor{blue}{Training each method until a fixed stopping rule on such validation data is met, and comparing the methods at those checkpoints, is left to future work.}

\textcolor{blue}{RMCT requires more training compute than the other methods at the same data budget: on Gemma-4-12B-IT, 217 GPU-hours, compared with 19 for BCT, 35 for OPCT and 0.34 for each activation-based method (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameters}).}

\textcolor{blue}{TBSR excludes responses without a parsed answer.} \textcolor{blue}{At most 3.5\% of any method's biased responses lack a parsed answer, compared with 0.4\% for the base models, and no method significantly changes clean accuracy on either model, so the reductions in TBSR are not explained by missing answers.} \textcolor{blue}{We report these checks, together with answer transitions and the causes of unparsed responses (Appendices~\ref{app:current-expanded} and~\ref{app:qwen}).}

\textcolor{blue}{We sample one response per question under each of the biased and unbiased prompts, so an observed switch may reflect sampling variance rather than the bias: the model may answer differently on two samples of the same prompt, with or without the cue.} \textcolor{blue}{Sampling each prompt repeatedly would allow estimating the switch rate expected from sampling variance alone, but multiplies the cost of evaluating each question, and so trades off against the number of questions evaluated at a fixed budget.}

Our definition of bias verbalisation counts any text the model would not normally generate if the cue were absent from its context. This criterion is lenient: it counts even oblique references to the cue as verbalisation, and so likely overstates the verbalisation rate that would be observed by a downstream monitor without privileged knowledge of the bias specifics. \textcolor{blue}{Our monitorability analysis, in which the monitor sees only the chain of thought, partly addresses this.}

Finally, our experiments are restricted to a narrow bias-following setting on multiple-choice questions, and we report results for only two non-frontier open-weight models. Future work will examine generalisation along both axes: of the method to other inconsistency problems, such as jailbreak susceptibility, persona drift, and evaluation gaming; and of the models to more capable, frontier-scale LLMs.

\section{Conclusion}
We introduce Rate Matching Consistency Training (RMCT), \textcolor{blue}{an outcomes-based consistency training method that enforces consistency only over an outcome of interest, here whether the final answer matches the bias.} \textcolor{blue}{Its reward depends only on that outcome, so it does not directly penalise verbalising the extraneous feature.} \textcolor{blue}{It also matches rates rather than responses, so it does not need a clean reference input.} \textcolor{blue}{On Gemma-4-12B-IT and Qwen3.5-9B, RMCT and five existing consistency training methods all reduce the towards-bias switch rate on held-out biases and a held-out dataset.} \textcolor{blue}{Beyond the multiple-choice format, the output-based methods, BCT, OPCT and RMCT, transfer further than the activation-based methods, most clearly to a free-text judgement task on Gemma-4-12B-IT, and BCT and OPCT transfer furthest, including to image-rendered questions on both models.} \textcolor{blue}{However, BCT and OPCT reduce both the verbalisation of the bias and chain-of-thought monitorability, whereas RMCT keeps both close to those of the base model.} \textcolor{blue}{The activation-based methods, ACT, AttCT and MLPCT, also do not significantly reduce monitorability at this training budget, but transfer least beyond the multiple-choice format.}
